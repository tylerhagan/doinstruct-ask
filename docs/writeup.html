Knowledge dead-ends are a routing problem, not a chatbot problem.

01What I got wrong

One wrong premise, and everything downstream inherited it

The first edition of this was built in a day and a half against a five-day brief, and it had a flaw I could not see from inside it. Rather than quietly fix it and hope nobody remembers, here is the flaw, because how someone handles being wrong is more useful information than how they handle being right.

The deployment model was mine, not yours

I reasoned that personal phones are often restricted in hygiene zones, therefore the realistic device is shared and wall-mounted, therefore there is no login, therefore no identity, therefore HR is out. That chain is coherent and its first link was wrong.

Your Learner page says "No app. No login. No barriers." Access is a QR code or a link in WhatsApp, SMS, Beekeeper or Flip. The Echterhoff study describes 670 construction workers completing briefings on personal smartphones. I had the answer available and inferred one instead.

The conclusion survived and the reasoning did not. No login still means no identity, and no identity still means HR is impossible to answer safely. It just rests on your published model now instead of on my guess, which matters because every cut I made hung off that premise, and a reader who knew your product would have spotted it in a sentence.

What it cost was not the conclusion, it was the texture. A wall-mounted terminal produced a gloved-hand, washdown-glare, one-device-for-everyone design that was really a design for one factory. It read as bespoke, because it was.

The second surface was not a cut, it was the missing half

I wrote, in the first edition: "The supervisor's side of the app. It's the obvious second surface, and building half of it would have demonstrated nothing." That was a defensible thing to say about scope and a wrong thing to believe about the product.

Without it, the thing serves one worker at one machine and shows nothing of the system around them. The whole argument is that a question becomes an asset, and the surface where that conversion actually happens was the one I did not build. It also left no room to show information architecture, data density or the judgement involved in refusing to build a feature, which is most of what a design engineer does.

Six smaller ones, in the order I found them

  • Language defaulted silently to Romanian. Your own flow is scan, pick a language, begin, and it is already familiar to every worker who has used the training product. The prototype now opens on the choice, and the picker takes a list rather than three fixed buttons, because three buttons quietly claim the product speaks three languages.
  • The device claimed to know the fault code. A sticker is static and cannot know today's fault, so showing E-212 as ambient context implied a live integration that does not exist. It now appears only when the URL carries it, which models a deep link from something that does know, and a plain scan shows the machine alone.
  • "No shadows" had quietly become "no depth". The floor's constraints are real and I applied them to a supervisor at a desk in an office, where none of them hold. That is what the second register in section 06 is for.
  • The brand was ink and never ground. Your identity is dark green fields with acid yellow on them. Mine was a cream document with black type and one yellow button, so it could not look like your product because it never made your most distinctive move.
  • tokens.json had been lying for weeks. Eleven of its eighteen colours were stale while its own header called it the source of truth. An agent reading it for intent would have been handed a palette the product had not used since the first day.
  • Two accessibility gaps I named rather than fixed. Focus never moved when the screen did, and the system used physical CSS properties while your language list includes Arabic and Dari. Naming a gap is better than hiding one and worse than closing it. Both are closed now, and the RTL migration turned out to be five properties.

The pattern underneath all of it

Every one of those was a rule this project had already written down. No shadows applies to the floor. Identifiers are never localised. Every icon is paired with text. The rules were right and they were prose, and prose decays.

Seven times now, a rule stated in a document has been broken by the person who wrote it. Every single one was caught by a human looking at the running product, until I started turning them into checks, and then a check caught one first. That is the argument of this whole case study in one sentence: a rule nothing tests is a rule with a half life.

There are four build checks and a test suite now, up from two. Each one is a sentence that used to be a promise.

02The slice

The environment picked the domain

You sell into food processing. You're listed as a member of the German meat and sausage producers' association, so this is a core vertical, not a guess on my part. That setting decides most of the important things before anyone draws a screen.

A worker arrives by scanning the QR code on the machine, on their own phone, or on a shared terminal in a hygiene zone where personal phones are restricted. Both are the same URL and the same product. Hands are wet and gloved either way, and your own principle, no app download and no passwords, means there is no login at either.

That is the entire access mechanism, and it is worth saying plainly because it is what makes the rest work: the QR identifies the machine, not the person. There is nothing to log in to because an asset ID is not a secret. It is painted on the nameplate and printed in the manual, so putting it in a URL gives away nothing the machine does not already show you.

Those constraints settled the scope for me, so I didn't have to have a preference:

I cut HR because it's private. No login means no identity. Payroll and holiday questions cannot be answered safely without one, and adding one breaks the no-password principle that gets this product adopted in the first place. Operational knowledge needs no identity at all, so it survives.

Voice is primary because wet, gloved hands can't type. Text stays as a degradation path, never an afterthought.

From there I narrowed once more, down to a single job: one technician, one shift, fault code E-212 on a filling line, German documentation, a Romanian speaker, a five-year-old Android.

What else I cut

  • Multi-turn conversation. Someone standing at a stopped machine needs one answer, not a dialogue. A follow-up is a new question.
  • The supervisor's side of the app. I cut this the first time and I was wrong; it is section 04 now. Building half of it would have demonstrated nothing, but not building it demonstrated less.
  • Onboarding and settings. A device reached by scanning a sticker has nobody to own preferences. Two controls survive: language and contrast. Language collapsed to a single chip once the picker moved to the front, because three buttons spend a 64px row of a 440px screen on a choice the worker made ten seconds ago.
  • Offline answering. The shell is cacheable but retrieval isn't. I'd rather name the gap than fake it.
03The product

An assistant that shrugs has rebuilt the dead-end

Your brief describes the problem precisely: "the answer is a colleague or supervisor away. So they wait." The knowledge isn't missing. It's sitting inside a person who isn't reachable right now.

So a system that answers seven questions in ten and apologises for the rest hasn't fixed anything. It has reproduced the original failure with better manners. The miss is the product. There are only three honest responses available:

The flow, including every escape

1Stage

Scan and choose

The QR on the machine resolves to Line 3, Filler F2, on a personal phone or a shared terminal. Language first, in its own script. No login, no download, and nothing typed by a gloved hand.

2Stage

Hold and ask

Hold to talk, never an open mic. The level meter is the only proof the device can hear you at 90 dB, and the room's noise floor is measured, not guessed at.

Escape: type instead. Always present, never buried.
3Stage

Confirm what was heard

Never auto-skipped on high confidence. Uncertain words are marked, and in the demo the marked word is the fault code, which is the token you least want wrong.

Escape: say it again, or switch to typing.

Then exactly one of three

1Response

Answer, with provenance

Grounded in a named document, section and page, with the document's age visible. Stale documentation is a hazard, and hiding its age quietly transfers that risk to the worker.

Escape: "this didn't help" drops straight into response 3.
2Response

Refuse, on safety

Guard-door bypass, lockout and tagout: an explicit stop, and a named qualified person. A refusal, not a hedged procedure wearing a disclaimer.

3Response

Route to a named human

Marek, shift lead, usually replies in about four minutes, speaks Romanian. A named person, an observed wait, not a promised one, and permission to walk away and be fetched.

Then: reply, capture, audit entry, and back into response 1.
Only the third branch loops, and that loop is the product. Marek's answer is captured, credited to the worker who asked, and written into the knowledge base with an audit entry attached. The next person to ask the same thing gets response 1 instead, in their own language. The wait happens once, and it produces an asset.

Three decisions in that flow are about people, not machines

Asking for help has a social cost. Admitting you don't know something in front of the person who writes the rota is expensive, and the usual result is that people stop asking. So when the assistant can't answer, it says "Das steht in keiner Anleitung", which puts the failure on the documentation, not on the worker. That's one line of copy, and it does more for whether this feature gets used than any amount of retrieval accuracy.

The wait is designed, not merely displayed. Marek is named, four minutes is an observed average, not a promise the system can break, his shift status is shown, and the worker is explicitly released to carry on working. Unexplained waits feel longer than explained ones, uncertain waits feel longer than bounded ones, and idle waiting feels longest of all. All three are fixable in copy, and none of them require the answer to arrive any sooner.

The end of the interaction credits the worker. Capture now says their question improved the manual before it says anything about the system. That moment is the emotional peak of the flow and the part that gets remembered, and it reframes needing help as having contributed. It's the cheapest lever available on whether someone asks a second time.

The same loop is the commercial argument. Every "this didn't help" is a gap in the knowledge base reported for free, and every captured answer emits an audit entry, which compounds into the compliance product you already sell rather than competing with it.

Where this sits next to Genius

Genius turns documents into knowledge. Ask retrieves that knowledge at the moment something breaks, and routes whatever is missing back into it. A sibling, not a rival, which is also why I didn't invent a second AI brand to sit alongside yours. The name is a placeholder and I'd expect you to overrule it.

Provenance and refusal are the same mechanism

Under time pressure, people over-trust automated advice, and a stopped line is precisely the condition that produces it. The counterweight isn't a disclaimer, because nobody reads disclaimers. It's two things that change what the worker actually does: a source they can open and check, and a system that visibly declines to answer some questions at all.

A tool that has said "no, get a qualified person" in front of you is a tool whose "yes" carries weight. Refusing well is how the rest of the answers earn being believed, which is why I'd resist any pressure to soften the stop state into a hedge.

The only place red appears in this document

An AI confidently describing a machine fix is a liability question before it's a UX question. Red is reserved for safety stop across the whole system, never for validation errors and never for "delete". That exclusivity is the point. A worker has to be able to trust that red means stop working.

04The office

The supervisor's scarce resource is their attention

Your own Echterhoff study says foremen spend excessive time on repetition instead of leading. That is the value proposition, and it is not a dashboard.

Today a shift lead answers the same question eleven times, because the answer lives in their head. This surface means they answer it once.

Four destinations, in order of urgency. Queue is unanswered questions, oldest first, and it is the only thing with a badge. Answer is where a reply becomes knowledge. Coverage is where the documentation runs out. Knowledge is the record, with provenance, for the compliance business you already sell.

Every row is someone waiting, and the queue says so

Each item carries what the assistant already tried, before the composer, never behind a disclosure. A supervisor answering blind will re-serve the fix the worker has already been given and rejected, which is worse than not answering. Where the assistant withheld an answer on safety grounds, the queue presents that as a success, because it is one: it had the answer and refused to give it. Framing that as a failure teaches supervisors to loosen the thing that is working.

If three people asked the same thing it appears once, with a count. That is a signal about the documentation and not about the three people, which is the distinction the whole surface turns on.

Coverage groups by what you can actually fix

There are only three fixable things behind an unanswered question, and each hands the supervisor a different next action: the documentation is unclear, so rewrite the section; the machine keeps producing the same fault, so it goes to engineering and no amount of rewriting will help; or nobody was trained, which is Genius and not this product. Grouping by asset or by document alone would have flattened three different problems into one list.

One shared bar scale across all three groups, so a group of small numbers cannot look like a crisis. Six weekly points is the floor for drawing a trend line at all: below that the component says there is too little data instead of drawing a confident line through noise, which would be the visual equivalent of the hallucination this product exists to avoid.

The line I would defend hardest, and it is a refusal

The first edition argued that asking for help carries a social cost, and worked hard to make asking survivable. All of that dies the moment a supervisor can see who asked what, and how often. But an absolute ban is product-naive: the system has to know who asked in order to tell them the answer.

Present-tense identity is operationally necessary. Historical accumulation is performance monitoring.

"Ana is waiting on an answer about Filler F2 right now" is a service queue and you cannot deliver without it. "Ana has asked 47 questions this quarter" is a competence record about a person, and nothing in this product needs one.

So the queue knows who is waiting, because it must. Coverage aggregates and never resolves to an individual. Once an answer is delivered, the link between the person and the question expires. Role, team and group are not offered as grouping dimensions at all, because they re-identify at small counts: slice by role and shift in a plant with one night-shift electrician and you have named them. Every bucket is suppressed below five events.

There is a legal edge here as well as an ethical one. In Germany, systems capable of monitoring individual employee performance fall under works council co-determination, §87(1)(6) BetrVG. That is a negotiation which takes months and can fail. Designing the accumulation out means a Betriebsrat conversation that takes one screen instead of one quarter, which is a sales-cycle feature rather than a compliance cost.

Two details make that argument visible rather than asserted. The Coverage screen states the refusal in words, on the page, so a supervisor who wonders why they cannot break this down by person finds the answer instead of concluding the feature is missing. And one bucket in the demo sits below the threshold and reads "too few to show", because a privacy rule nobody can see working is a privacy rule nobody believes.

It is also enforced. The queue fixtures are asserted in the test suite to carry no field on the asker beyond a name and a role. Add questionsThisMonth and the build fails.

What I deliberately did not build here

  • Any per-person view. Covered above. Its absence is the argument, not an omission.
  • Authoring of training courses. That is Genius. Competing with your own product inside a case study for your company would be a misread.
  • Org chart and role management. The Manager product already mirrors the org chart. This surface consumes that; it does not rebuild it.
  • Real retrieval. Still fixtures. Faking a RAG pipeline would teach you nothing, and the honest gap is more useful than a convincing mock.

See it hold up, rather than take my word for it

Every condition below is switchable in the prototype. The controls sit behind the Demo button at the bottom right, which is deliberately styled as scaffolding so nobody mistakes it for shipped UI.

  • A low-end device. Open devtools, throttle to slow 3G and reload. There are no web fonts to wait for and no server round trip, so first paint does not depend on the network finishing.
  • A noisy line. Demo, then Loud room. The level meter turns to caution and the interface offers the text path before recognition fails rather than after.
  • Washdown glare. Demo, then Washdown glare. Every border goes to 2px black, surfaces go white, decorative fills strip out. It is a lighting condition, not a preference.
  • Hands busy. Run the first scenario, tick a step, then move away and come back. The tick persists, because the device holds the state the worker cannot. Read-aloud covers the case where eyes are committed too.
  • Another language. Switch to Deutsch or English in the top bar. The procedure steps change, not just the buttons, and the source chip discloses that the manual stayed German.
  • Low digital literacy is the one that shows by absence. No login, no download, no onboarding, no settings screen. There is nothing to learn before you can ask a question.

What a stopped line does to attention

Acute stress narrows working memory. The person using this has a line down, a supervisor forming an opinion, and a procedure to follow, and they are the least able to hold a five-step sequence in their head at exactly the moment they most need to.

So the device holds the state instead. Steps are ticked and the ticks persist, because a technician reads a step, puts the device down, does the work with both hands, and comes back thirty seconds later needing to know where they were. The next step is visually dominant and completed ones recede without disappearing, since verifying what you already did is part of the work. One instruction per step, imperative, with the reason after the instruction rather than before it, so the sentence survives being read alone and in a hurry.

Noise, at 85 to 95 dB

The room's noise floor is measured, not assumed. The first thirty frames of audio establish it, and above the threshold the interface offers the text path before recognition fails rather than after it.

Confirmation of what was heard is mandatory and never auto-skipped on high confidence. Mis-recognition is normal in this environment, and a technician acting on a misheard fault code is the exact failure this product exists to prevent. In the demo, the word marked uncertain is the fault code itself.

Hold to talk, which isn't a styling choice

A German works council will not approve a device that listens passively. Holding to talk makes "this is not recording you" a physical property of the control instead of a policy promise. It also happens to be more forgiving with gloves than a second precise tap, and it bounds the cost of each utterance. Three arguments, one gesture.

The part of this I'm least comfortable with

Voice is public. On an open floor, asking a question out loud broadcasts what you don't know to whoever is standing nearby, and that can include the supervisor whose opinion sets your shifts. Everything above about the social cost of asking applies double when the asking is audible, and it cuts against the modality I chose as primary.

The text path is the honest answer, which is why it's never buried and why the interface offers it before recognition fails, not after. But it's a mitigation, not a solution, and a usability test in a quiet room would never surface the problem at all. If this went further, the thing I'd want to watch is not accuracy but who asks and when. I'd expect the asking rate to drop when a supervisor is on shift, and that one measurement would tell you more about whether this works than any recognition benchmark.

Language isn't translation

Switching language changes the answer itself, down to the procedure steps, not just the buttons. Language names appear in their own script, because someone scanning for their language doesn't read the interface language. When the answer is Romanian and the manual is German, the source chip says so, so opening a German PDF is never a surprise.

The German copy uses the informal "du" deliberately. These are colleagues on a line rather than customers, and "Sie" reads as management talking at them.

And some things stay German on purpose. "Füller F2", "AST-3121", "E-212" and the manual's title never translate, in any locale, because the worker has to match them against a nameplate, an HMI screen and a document on a shelf. Translating the machine name means the screen and the machine disagree, and the worker has no way to tell which is right. The worst case is someone working on the wrong machine. Descriptors go the other way: the shift lead's job title is localised, because it tells the worker whether this person can help and it isn't printed on anything.

That distinction is now in the domain model as Identifier versus Localised, so the type says which one you meant. Worth noting that neither of my checks catches it, because these strings live in the data layer where a naive rule would flag exactly the ones that must stay German. It's a judgement call, so it's written down instead.

06The system

Why agents are bad at your stack specifically

I think this is the actual job, so it's where I spent most of the thinking.

Svelte 5 runes shipped in late 2024, and Tailwind v4's CSS-first config in early 2025. Both replaced the APIs that dominate the training data every model learned from. Left unconstrained, an agent writes confident, plausible, wrong code in your stack: Svelte 4 stores and export let, and a tailwind.config.js that doesn't exist here and is silently ignored.

So the goal of agents producing more than 95% good UI isn't failing because agents are bad at UI. It's failing because they're good at the previous version of your stack. That's a rules problem, and rules are fixable.

AGENTS.md is the deliverable: exact versions, a Svelte 4 to 5 translation table, the token contract, and nine non-negotiables that encode safety and accessibility, not taste. One structural decision does most of the work. Components are dumb; they render props and report gestures, and the state machine lives in one route. So "change the flow" touches one file, "restyle the answer" touches another, and neither breaks the other.

The rules are split across four files instead of one, and the split is itself a design decision about working with agents. A rules file long enough to cover everything gets skimmed by humans and diluted in a long session, so every rule you add makes the others slightly less likely to be followed. AGENTS.md holds what applies always, and indexes three focused files with a stated trigger for each: conduct rules for any code change, copy rules when text is involved, accessibility rules when an interactive element is. Telling an agent when to load something is context budgeting made explicit.

The rules I'd point at first aren't the visual ones. The dependency list is closed, because one convenience library quietly destroys the 46 KB budget that every claim in the previous section rests on, and nothing would catch it. And never weaken a check to make it pass, which matters now that there are checks worth protecting. An agent that can't reach 7:1 will lower the threshold instead of fixing the colour, and the result compiles, passes, and is a lie.

I chose Svelte having never shipped it

Worth saying plainly. I've built in React for years. I picked SvelteKit because a React design system would make your fourth requirement impossible for your team, and because the interesting problem turned out to be the gap described above. The weakness of the tooling is the reason the work matters, not an obstacle to it.

The handover, tested

Three prompts, each committed separately, so the diffs are inspectable in git history and not described in prose. The first two I ran myself, which means they only prove I can follow my own instructions. The third was run cold, by an agent that knew nothing about this repository, this document, or the fact that anyone would look at the result. It got four sentences and nothing else. Its raw output is on the handover-prompt-3 branch, committed with no edits from me.

Prompt 1, adding a procedure step in three languages: one file, five lines added, zero errors, no polish needed. The localised content type made a German-only step impossible to compile.

Prompt 2, adding a whole new phase to the flow: two files, thirty lines, zero errors, working feature. It composed entirely from existing primitives, with no new component, no new token and no raw values.

What the polish pass caught, which is the interesting part

The agent added a "Back" control with a hardcoded German string:

- <Button onclick={() => (phase = 'answer')}>Zurück</Button>
+ <Button onclick={() => (phase = 'answer')}>{t('flow.back')}</Button>

It had followed the instruction it was given, "add all copy to strings.ts", for the copy the prompt actually named. Then it invented an unnamed control and fell back to its default habit for that one. When I went looking, I found three more violations of the same rule sitting in my own baseline. The agent's slip was a smaller version of mine.

Which makes me think 95% is the wrong way to frame the target. Roughly 95% of the lines were right, but the missing part wasn't randomly distributed. It was precisely the thing that only matters if you know the domain. An untranslated button compiles perfectly, passes every automated check, is invisible in review, and is a dead end for half the workforce on a German line.

That's a useful result, not a disappointing one. It says the design engineer's job here isn't drawing screens or reviewing syntax. It's writing the rules, auditing the exact class of thing that rules can't yet enforce, and then shrinking that class.

Then the cold run said the same thing, without my help

Prompt 3 restyled EscalationCard so the responder's name and expected wait became dominant. The mechanical layer was faultless: token classes only, no raw values, every prop and aria attribute preserved, existing translation keys reused instead of new strings invented, exactly the one file touched, and both checks passing on the first attempt. I verified all of that myself instead of trusting its summary. Six lines added, four removed.

Three things it got wrong, none of which any check can see.

It turned an estimate into a promise. "Antwortet normalerweise in ~4 Min." is now a 28px headline, the largest thing on the card. That number is an observed average, and the escalation flow works precisely because it doesn't read as a commitment the system can fail. The copy was worded carefully to avoid a claim, and the typography made it anyway.

It demoted the line doing the most work. "Das steht in keiner Anleitung" dropped from body text to small and muted. That's the line that puts the failure on the documentation instead of the worker, which is what makes asking survivable in front of colleagues. It's now the quietest thing in the card.

It put a status in 14px, which rule 2 reserves for timestamps and audit references. Nothing caught it, because one script checks colour and the other checks literal strings. Neither checks type size.

And it caught me. Auditing that third point showed rule 2 contradicted my own token file: the rule banned anything under 18px, while tokens.json defines a 16px size for labels, which my own baseline used. The agent wasn't breaking a clear rule, it was obeying an unclear one. Rule 2 now says what it always meant.

So the same pattern held with nobody's thumb on the scale. Everything a machine can verify was correct immediately. Everything requiring knowledge of why a sentence was phrased that way needed a person. The useful number isn't 95%. It's which 5%, and it lands in the same place every time.

So I started shrinking it

AGENTS.md claimed "no text token below 7:1 exists". That was prose, which meant it stopped being true the moment anyone changed a colour. I replaced it with a script that fails the build.

It immediately caught five colour pairs that didn't meet the rule I'd written, four of them my own status colours sitting between 5.4:1 and 6.1:1. I'd asserted a standard in a document and then not met it, and no amount of re-reading had noticed.

Worth being exact about what the script did and didn't do, since being exact is the entire reason for writing it. Two of your brand swatches also fall below the floor, and I'll come to those below, but I caught those by hand while adopting the palette, before the script existed. The script found five things I had got wrong. It gets credit for five. 

pass   15.64:1  (min 7)  ink on yellow    label on the primary voice action
pass    8.21:1  (min 7)  stop on stop-surface   safety stop text
pass    3.06:1  (min 3)  border on surface      default border against page

All contrast checks passed.

So I did the same to the i18n rule, which is the one the agent had broken. check-i18n.mjs refuses any literal text in component markup outside t(). It found four more defects within seconds of existing, all of them mine, and all of them German aria-label values.

That last detail is the one I'd point at. Those labels are invisible on screen, compile perfectly, and pass every other check. What they do is read out German to a Romanian speaker using a screen reader, inside a product whose entire argument is that language should not be a barrier. I had written the rule, broken it four times, and not noticed until a twenty-line script told me.

Every rule in AGENTS.md that can become a check should become one. Prose rules degrade as an agent's context fills up. A failing build doesn't.

There are four now, and the third one is the one that changed my mind about the whole approach. check-tokens.mjs asserts that every colour in tokens.json exists in tokens.css and the reverse, and the first time it ran it found that eleven of the eighteen colours in the file calling itself the source of truth were stale: a first-draft palette the product had not used in weeks. Nothing had caught it because "source of truth" was a sentence rather than a condition.

That was the first defect in this project found by something other than a person looking at the running product. Every previous one, including all of mine, was found by eye. check-register.mjs is the fourth, and it enforces the boundary between the two registers in section 06: the office may use anything the floor uses, the floor may never use an office token. One direction, mechanically checkable. I injected a violation and watched it fail before I believed it, because a check that has never failed is a check nobody has tested.

There are 43 tests as well, which meant narrowing a rule rather than breaking it. The dependency rule said package.json is closed, full stop, and it existed to protect 46 KB of JavaScript on a five-year-old Android. It was also banning the tooling that keeps every other rule honest, and a devDependency never reaches the device. So it now reads: no new runtime dependency. A date library is still banned and a charting library emphatically still is.

The test I would defend hardest is structural rather than behavioural. The queue fixtures are asserted to carry no field on the asker beyond a name and a role. Add questionsThisMonth and the suite fails. That moves the surveillance boundary out of a paragraph in a document and into the build.

Where that argument stops, which matters just as much

Late in this work I introduced three defects in the high-contrast theme and the device frame. A blanket CSS rule handed a visible border to every paragraph on the page. The rounded frame failed to clip its children. And two design tokens collided on a name, so a width silently resolved to the wrong value and rendered at about twice its intended size.

All three compiled without complaint and passed every check I'd just built. Two of them only appear in a theme you have to deliberately toggle into. They were found by looking at the running product on a phone, and nothing else would have found them.

So the honest version of my argument is narrower than "turn the rules into checks". It is: automate every rule you can, write down the ones you can't, and keep looking at the real thing. If I'd only made the first claim I'd have been doing the same thing the agent did, which is sounding confident about something I hadn't verified.

On your brand, since I read it instead of guessing

I pulled the swatch definitions out of your production stylesheet, so the palette here uses your exact values, not my approximations of them. Two of them needed adjusting for this context, and I've recorded both in tokens.json with the reasoning: --swatch--mid-green measures 4.6:1 on your off-white, and --swatch--light-green measures 1.65:1, which is invisible as a structural border. Both are perfectly good marketing values. Neither survives washdown glare. I darkened both and kept the hue.

I've also used your own name for the accent. Your system calls it yellow, not lime, so this one does too.

The typeface is the one decision I made, defended, and then reversed. The first edition shipped a system font stack and zero font bytes, on the reasoning that a web font is a request that can fail and a layout shift landing in front of someone whose line is stopped. That reasoning was sound and the result was characterless: on Windows the system stack resolves to Segoe UI, and it was the single biggest reason this looked like a wireframe rather than like your product.

It ships Manrope now, self-hosted, subset and variable, and the thing that makes the reversal safe is font-display: optional. The browser gets a short window; if it misses, the page renders in the fallback and never swaps, so there is no reflow at any point in the load. Paired with a preload, a reviewer on a decent connection gets Manrope on first paint and a worker on plant wifi gets the system stack immediately. Nobody waits for text and nothing moves.

24.3 KB for Latin, and 14.8 KB more only if the reader is Romanian, Polish, Turkish or Hungarian. Self-hosted rather than linked from Google, because a third-party font request is a third-party DNS lookup, a second TLS handshake and a privacy question in a German B2B sale, none of which is worth it for two files served from the same origin as everything else. One family across both registers: the office does not get its own typeface, because a two-register system with two typefaces is two systems.
