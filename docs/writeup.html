01 The slice

The environment picks the domain

doinstruct sells into food processing — you're a member of the German meat and sausage producers' association, so this is a core vertical rather than a guess. That setting decides almost everything before a single screen gets drawn.

In a hygiene zone, personal phones are frequently restricted. Hands are wet and gloved. The realistic device is shared and wall-mounted. And your own product principle — no app download, no passwords — means that device has no login.

Which settles the scope without me having to have a preference:

HR is cut because it's private. On a shared, no-login device, payroll and vacation questions need per-worker authentication, and that breaks the no-password principle. Operational knowledge isn't private, so it survives on a shared device.

Voice is primary because gloved, wet hands can't type. Text stays as a degradation path, never an afterthought.

Then I narrowed once more, to a single job: one technician, one shift, fault code E-212 on a filling line, German documentation, Romanian speaker, five-year-old Android.

What else I cut

  • Multi-turn conversation. A technician mid-fault needs one answer, not a dialogue. Follow-up is a new question.
  • The supervisor's side of the app. It's the obvious second surface, and building half of it would have proved nothing.
  • Onboarding and settings. A shared device has nobody to own preferences. Two controls survive: language and contrast.
  • Offline answering. The shell is cacheable; retrieval isn't. I'd rather name the gap than fake it.
02 The product

An assistant that shrugs has rebuilt the dead-end

Your brief describes the problem precisely: "the answer is a colleague or supervisor away. So they wait." The knowledge isn't missing. It's in a person who isn't reachable right now.

So a system that answers 70% of questions and apologises for the rest hasn't solved anything — it's reproduced the original failure with better manners. The miss is the product. There are only three honest responses:

Response 1

Answer, with provenance

Grounded in a named document, section and page, with the document's age visible. Stale documentation is a hazard, and hiding its age transfers that risk to the worker silently.

Response 2

Refuse, on safety

Guard-door bypass, lockout/tagout: an explicit stop and a named qualified person. A refusal, never a hedged procedure wearing a disclaimer.

Response 3

Route to a named human

Marek, shift lead, usually replies in ~4 minutes, speaks Romanian. His answer is then captured — and the next person who asks gets it instantly, in their language.

That third branch is the whole commercial argument. The wait happens once, and it produces an asset. Every "this didn't help" tap is a gap in the knowledge base being reported for free — and every captured answer emits an audit entry, which compounds into the compliance product you already sell rather than competing with it.

Where this sits next to Genius

Genius turns documents into knowledge. Ask retrieves it at the moment of failure, and routes what's missing back into it. Sibling, not rival — which is also why I didn't invent a second AI brand for it.

The one place red appears in this document

An AI confidently describing a machine fix is a liability question before it's a UX question. Red is reserved for safety stop across the entire system — never validation errors, never "delete". That exclusivity is the point: a worker has to be able to trust that red means stop working.

Noise, at 85–95 dB

The room's noise floor is measured, not assumed — the first 30 frames of audio establish it, and above threshold the interface offers the text path before recognition fails rather than after. The level meter isn't decoration either: in a loud room it's the only evidence the device can hear you.

And confirmation is mandatory, never auto-skipped on high confidence. Mis-recognition is the norm here, and a technician acting on a misheard fault code is the exact failure this product exists to prevent. In the demo, the word marked uncertain is the fault code itself.

Hold-to-talk, and why it isn't a styling choice

A German works council will not approve a device that listens passively. Hold-to-talk makes "this is not recording you" a physical property of the control rather than a policy promise. It also happens to be more forgiving with gloves than a second precise tap, and it bounds the cost per utterance. Three arguments, one gesture.

Language isn't translation

Switching language changes the answer, down to the procedure steps — not just the buttons. Language names appear in their own script, because someone scanning for their language doesn't read the interface language. And when the answer is Romanian and the manual is German, the source chip says so, so opening a German PDF is never a surprise.

The German copy is deliberately informal du. These are colleagues on a line, not customers; Sie reads as management talking at them.

04 The system

Why agents are bad at your stack, specifically

This is the part I'd argue is the actual job, so it's where I spent the most thought.

Svelte 5 runes shipped in late 2024; Tailwind v4's CSS-first config in early 2025. Both replaced the APIs that dominate every model's training data. Left unconstrained, an agent writes confident, plausible, wrong code in this stack — Svelte 4 stores and export let, and a tailwind.config.js that doesn't exist here and is silently ignored.

So the goal of agents producing >95% good UI isn't failing because agents are bad at UI. It's failing because they're good at the previous version of your stack. That's a rules problem, and rules are fixable.

AGENTS.md is the deliverable: exact versions, a Svelte-4-to-5 translation table, the token contract, and nine hard rules that encode safety and accessibility rather than taste. One structural decision does most of the work — components are dumb. They render props and report gestures; the state machine lives in one route. So "change the flow" touches one file and "restyle the answer" touches another, and neither breaks the other.

I chose Svelte having never shipped it

Worth saying plainly. I've built in React for years. I picked SvelteKit because a React design system would make your fourth requirement impossible for your team — and because the interesting problem turned out to be exactly the gap above. The weakness of the tooling is why the work matters, not an obstacle to it.

The handover, tested

I ran the prompts and committed each result separately, so the diffs are inspectable in git history rather than described.

Prompt 1 (add a procedure step, three languages): 1 file, +5 lines, zero errors, no polish needed. The localised content type made a German-only step impossible to compile.

Prompt 2 (add a whole new phase to the flow): 2 files, +30 lines, zero errors, works. It composed entirely from existing primitives — no new component, no new token, no raw values.

What the polish pass caught, which is the interesting part

The agent added a "Back" button with a hardcoded German string:

- <Button onclick={() => (phase = 'answer')}>Zurück</Button>
+ <Button onclick={() => (phase = 'answer')}>{t('flow.back')}</Button>

It had followed the instruction it was given — "add all copy to strings.ts" — for the copy the prompt named, then invented an unnamed control and reverted to its default habit for that one. Auditing for it turned up three pre-existing violations of the same rule in my own baseline. The agent's slip was a smaller version of mine.

Which makes me think >95% is the wrong frame. About 95% of the lines were right, but the missing 5% wasn't randomly distributed — it was precisely the thing that only matters if you know the domain. An untranslated button compiles perfectly, passes every automated check, is invisible in review, and is a dead end for half the workforce on a German line.

That's a useful result, not a disappointing one. It says the design engineer's job here isn't drawing screens or reviewing syntax. It's writing the rules, then auditing the exact class of thing rules can't yet enforce — and then shrinking that class.

So the next move is cheap and obvious

A lint rule banning literal text in Svelte markup outside t() would have caught all four instances — the agent's and my three — at commit time. Every rule in AGENTS.md that can become a check should become one. Prose rules degrade as an agent's context fills up; a failing build doesn't. The prose file is where that migration starts, not where it ends.

05 Honestly

What I'd want to pressure-test with you

  • The handover test wasn't cold. I wrote both the rules and the code they govern. A genuinely cold run — different engineer, fresh session — is the real proof, and it's the first thing I'd want to do together rather than assert here.
  • ASR is the largest technical risk and I can't honestly prototype it. Romanian-accented German over machine noise is where this product succeeds or fails. The demo carries a scripted mode alongside the live mic for exactly that reason — I'd rather show you the seam than hide it.
  • I've assumed the wall-mounted shared device. It follows from hygiene-zone phone restrictions, but you have the customer contact and I don't. If it's wrong, the HR cut reverses and a lot follows.
  • Capture needs a verification policy I haven't designed. One shift lead's answer becoming canonical knowledge is how you get fast institutional learning, and also how you'd propagate a confidently wrong fix.

Colophon. This document follows the same rules as the system it describes. Red appears exactly once, on the safety note. Lime appears exactly once — here. Hierarchy comes from rules and surface rather than shadow, because shadows are invisible under washdown lighting and this system is meant to survive being printed to a laminated card for the line. Nothing animates.

Set in system sans, Georgia, and a monospace stack for references — zero webfont bytes, for the same reason the product carries none.
