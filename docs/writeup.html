Knowledge dead-ends are a routing problem, not a chatbot problem.

A voice-first way for a maintenance technician to get an answer mid-shift, and, when there is no answer, to reach the person who has it. Built in your stack, with the rules an AI agent needs in order to keep building it.

01 The slice

The environment picked the domain

You sell into food processing. You're listed as a member of the German meat and sausage producers' association, so this is a core vertical rather than a guess on my part. That setting decides most of the important things before anyone draws a screen.

In a hygiene zone, personal phones are often restricted. Hands are wet and gloved. The realistic device is shared and wall-mounted. And your own product principle, no app download and no passwords, means that device has no login.

Those constraints settled the scope for me, so I didn't have to have a preference:

I cut HR because it's private. On a shared device with no login, payroll and holiday questions need per-worker authentication, and that breaks the no-password principle. Operational knowledge isn't private, so it survives on a shared device.

Voice is primary because wet, gloved hands can't type. Text stays as a degradation path rather than an afterthought.

From there I narrowed once more, down to a single job: one technician, one shift, fault code E-212 on a filling line, German documentation, a Romanian speaker, a five-year-old Android.

What else I cut

  • Multi-turn conversation. Someone standing at a stopped machine needs one answer, not a dialogue. A follow-up is a new question.
  • The supervisor's side of the app. It's the obvious second surface, and building half of it would have demonstrated nothing.
  • Onboarding and settings. A shared device has nobody to own preferences. Two controls survive: language and contrast.
  • Offline answering. The shell is cacheable but retrieval isn't. I'd rather name the gap than fake it.
02 The product

An assistant that shrugs has rebuilt the dead-end

Your brief describes the problem precisely: "the answer is a colleague or supervisor away. So they wait." The knowledge isn't missing. It's sitting inside a person who isn't reachable right now.

So a system that answers seven questions in ten and apologises for the rest hasn't fixed anything. It has reproduced the original failure with better manners. The miss is the product. There are only three honest responses available:

Response 1

Answer, with provenance

Grounded in a named document, section and page, with the document's age visible. Stale documentation is a hazard, and hiding its age quietly transfers that risk to the worker.

Response 2

Refuse, on safety

Guard-door bypass, lockout and tagout: an explicit stop, and a named qualified person. A refusal rather than a hedged procedure wearing a disclaimer.

Response 3

Route to a named human

Marek, shift lead, usually replies in about four minutes, speaks Romanian. His answer is then captured, so the next person to ask gets it instantly, in their own language.

That third branch is the commercial argument. The wait happens once and it produces an asset. Every "this didn't help" tap is a gap in the knowledge base being reported for free, and every captured answer emits an audit entry, which compounds into the compliance product you already sell rather than competing with it.

Where this sits next to Genius

Genius turns documents into knowledge. Ask retrieves that knowledge at the moment something breaks, and routes whatever is missing back into it. A sibling rather than a rival, which is also why I didn't invent a second AI brand to sit alongside yours. The name is a placeholder and I'd expect you to overrule it.

The only place red appears in this document

An AI confidently describing a machine fix is a liability question before it's a UX question. Red is reserved for safety stop across the whole system, never for validation errors and never for "delete". That exclusivity is the point. A worker has to be able to trust that red means stop working.

03 Frontline reality

Measured rather than asserted

You can reproduce any of these with npm run build.

Measure Value Why it's that number
JavaScript, gzipped 48 KB Static and prerendered, with no server round trip before first paint.
CSS, gzipped 4 KB Token-driven, with no unused framework surface.
Web font bytes 0 Covered below. Losing a typeface I control is a real cost, but a technician staring at invisible text while a line is down is a larger one.
Minimum tap target 64 px WCAG 2.2 AAA asks for 44px, which assumes a bare fingertip. A cut-resistant glove has a contact patch of roughly 14 to 16mm and no proprioceptive feedback.
Push-to-talk target 96 px Hittable without looking, while holding a part in the other hand.
Minimum body text 18 px Read at arm's length from a wall mount, often through safety glasses. 14px exists only for timestamps and audit references, never for instructions.
Minimum text contrast 7:1 AA's 4.5:1 isn't enough under washdown glare or in a dim cold store. Enforced by a script, not by good intentions.

Noise, at 85 to 95 dB

The room's noise floor is measured rather than assumed. The first thirty frames of audio establish it, and above the threshold the interface offers the text path before recognition fails rather than after it.

Confirmation of what was heard is mandatory and never auto-skipped on high confidence. Mis-recognition is normal in this environment, and a technician acting on a misheard fault code is the exact failure this product exists to prevent. In the demo, the word marked uncertain is the fault code itself.

Hold to talk, which isn't a styling choice

A German works council will not approve a device that listens passively. Holding to talk makes "this is not recording you" a physical property of the control rather than a policy promise. It also happens to be more forgiving with gloves than a second precise tap, and it bounds the cost of each utterance. Three arguments, one gesture.

Language isn't translation

Switching language changes the answer itself, down to the procedure steps, not just the buttons. Language names appear in their own script, because someone scanning for their language doesn't read the interface language. When the answer is Romanian and the manual is German, the source chip says so, so opening a German PDF is never a surprise.

The German copy uses the informal "du" deliberately. These are colleagues on a line rather than customers, and "Sie" reads as management talking at them.

04 The system

Why agents are bad at your stack specifically

I think this is the actual job, so it's where I spent most of the thinking.

Svelte 5 runes shipped in late 2024, and Tailwind v4's CSS-first config in early 2025. Both replaced the APIs that dominate the training data every model learned from. Left unconstrained, an agent writes confident, plausible, wrong code in your stack: Svelte 4 stores and export let, and a tailwind.config.js that doesn't exist here and is silently ignored.

So the goal of agents producing more than 95% good UI isn't failing because agents are bad at UI. It's failing because they're good at the previous version of your stack. That's a rules problem, and rules are fixable.

AGENTS.md is the deliverable: exact versions, a Svelte 4 to 5 translation table, the token contract, and nine non-negotiables that encode safety and accessibility rather than taste. One structural decision does most of the work. Components are dumb; they render props and report gestures, and the state machine lives in one route. So "change the flow" touches one file, "restyle the answer" touches another, and neither breaks the other.

The rules are split across four files rather than one, and the split is itself a design decision about working with agents. A rules file long enough to cover everything gets skimmed by humans and diluted in a long session, so every rule you add makes the others slightly less likely to be followed. AGENTS.md holds what applies always, and indexes three focused files with a stated trigger for each: conduct rules for any code change, copy rules when text is involved, accessibility rules when an interactive element is. Telling an agent when to load something is context budgeting made explicit.

The rules I'd point at first aren't the visual ones. The dependency list is closed, because one convenience library quietly destroys the 48 KB budget that every claim in the previous section rests on, and nothing would catch it. And never weaken a check to make it pass, which matters now that there are checks worth protecting. An agent that can't reach 7:1 will lower the threshold rather than fix the colour, and the result compiles, passes, and is a lie.

I chose Svelte having never shipped it

Worth saying plainly. I've built in React for years. I picked SvelteKit because a React design system would make your fourth requirement impossible for your team, and because the interesting problem turned out to be the gap described above. The weakness of the tooling is the reason the work matters rather than an obstacle to it.

The handover, tested

I ran the prompts and committed each result separately, so the diffs are inspectable in git history rather than described in prose.

Prompt 1, adding a procedure step in three languages: one file, five lines added, zero errors, no polish needed. The localised content type made a German-only step impossible to compile.

Prompt 2, adding a whole new phase to the flow: two files, thirty lines, zero errors, working feature. It composed entirely from existing primitives, with no new component, no new token and no raw values.

What the polish pass caught, which is the interesting part

The agent added a "Back" control with a hardcoded German string:

- <Button onclick={() => (phase = 'answer')}>Zurück</Button>
+ <Button onclick={() => (phase = 'answer')}>{t('flow.back')}</Button>

It had followed the instruction it was given, "add all copy to strings.ts", for the copy the prompt actually named. Then it invented an unnamed control and fell back to its default habit for that one. When I went looking, I found three more violations of the same rule sitting in my own baseline. The agent's slip was a smaller version of mine.

Which makes me think 95% is the wrong way to frame the target. Roughly 95% of the lines were right, but the missing part wasn't randomly distributed. It was precisely the thing that only matters if you know the domain. An untranslated button compiles perfectly, passes every automated check, is invisible in review, and is a dead end for half the workforce on a German line.

That's a useful result rather than a disappointing one. It says the design engineer's job here isn't drawing screens or reviewing syntax. It's writing the rules, auditing the exact class of thing that rules can't yet enforce, and then shrinking that class.

So I started shrinking it

AGENTS.md claimed "no text token below 7:1 exists". That was prose, which meant it stopped being true the moment anyone changed a colour. I replaced it with a script that fails the build.

It immediately caught five colour pairs that didn't meet the rule I'd written, including four of my own status colours sitting between 5.4:1 and 6.1:1. I'd asserted a standard in a document and then not met it, and no human review had noticed. It also found two of your brand swatches that don't clear the floor on a plant floor, which I'll come to below. 

pass   15.64:1  (min 7)  ink on yellow    label on the primary voice action
pass    8.21:1  (min 7)  stop on stop-surface   safety stop text
pass    3.06:1  (min 3)  border on surface      default border against page

All contrast checks passed.

So I did the same to the i18n rule, which is the one the agent had broken. check-i18n.mjs refuses any literal text in component markup outside t(). It found four more defects within seconds of existing, all of them mine, and all of them German aria-label values.

That last detail is the one I'd point at. Those labels are invisible on screen, compile perfectly, and pass every other check. What they do is read out German to a Romanian speaker using a screen reader, inside a product whose entire argument is that language should not be a barrier. I had written the rule, broken it four times, and not noticed until a twenty-line script told me.

Every rule in AGENTS.md that can become a check should become one. Prose rules degrade as an agent's context fills up. A failing build doesn't. Two are done and npm run check now runs both.

Where that argument stops, which matters just as much

Late in this work I introduced three defects in the high-contrast theme and the device frame. A blanket CSS rule handed a visible border to every paragraph on the page. The rounded frame failed to clip its children. And two design tokens collided on a name, so a width silently resolved to the wrong value and rendered at about twice its intended size.

All three compiled without complaint and passed every check I'd just built. Two of them only appear in a theme you have to deliberately toggle into. They were found by looking at the running product on a phone, and nothing else would have found them.

So the honest version of my argument is narrower than "turn the rules into checks". It is: automate every rule you can, write down the ones you can't, and keep looking at the real thing. If I'd only made the first claim I'd have been doing the same thing the agent did, which is sounding confident about something I hadn't verified.

On your brand, since I read it rather than guessed

I pulled the swatch definitions out of your production stylesheet, so the palette here uses your exact values rather than my approximations of them. Two of them needed adjusting for this context, and I've recorded both in tokens.json with the reasoning: --swatch--mid-green measures 4.6:1 on your off-white, and --swatch--light-green measures 1.65:1, which is invisible as a structural border. Both are perfectly good marketing values. Neither survives washdown glare. I darkened both and kept the hue.

I've also used your own name for the accent. Your system calls it yellow rather than lime, so this one does too.

The one place I've knowingly walked away from your brand is the typeface. You use Manrope; this uses a system font stack and ships zero font bytes. On a five-year-old Android over plant wifi, a web font is a request that can fail and a layout shift that lands in front of someone whose line is stopped. The system stack is guaranteed to be present and costs nothing. I'd rather be off-brand on purpose than have your typeface fail to arrive.

05 Honestly

What I'd want to pressure-test with you

  • The handover test wasn't cold. I wrote both the rules and the code they govern. A genuinely cold run, with a different engineer and a fresh session, is the real proof, and it's the first thing I'd want to do together rather than assert here.
  • Speech recognition is the largest technical risk and I can't honestly prototype it. Romanian-accented German over machine noise is where this product succeeds or fails. The demo carries a scripted mode alongside the live microphone for exactly that reason. I'd rather show you the seam than hide it.
  • I've assumed a shared, wall-mounted device. It follows from hygiene-zone phone restrictions, but you have the customer contact and I don't. If that assumption is wrong, the HR cut reverses and a good deal follows from it.
  • Capture needs a verification policy I haven't designed. One shift lead's answer becoming canonical knowledge is how you get fast institutional learning. It's also how you'd propagate a confidently wrong fix to everyone at once.
  • This isn't right-to-left ready, and your language list says it should be. You advertise 35+ languages, and that list includes Arabic and Dari. My components use physical CSS properties rather than logical ones, so the layout would not mirror. The rule is now in docs/rules/accessibility.md so new work doesn't deepen it, but the existing migration is outstanding. I found this late and chose to name it rather than rush it the day before sending.
  • Focus isn't managed across phase changes. Moving between screens leaves a keyboard or screen reader user on a control that no longer exists, which is a dead end inside a product about removing dead ends. Also worth flagging that the European Accessibility Act has applied since June 2025, so for a German B2B seller this is a procurement question rather than only an ethical one.

Colophon. This document follows the same rules as the system it describes. Red appears exactly once, on the safety note. Yellow appears exactly once, here. Hierarchy comes from rules and surface rather than shadow, because shadows are invisible under washdown lighting and this system is meant to survive being printed onto a laminated card for the line. Nothing animates.

Set in a system sans, Georgia, and a monospace stack for references. Zero web font bytes, for the same reason the product carries none.
